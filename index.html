    Two things move the end date more than anything else. The first is long-lead material: cabinetry and custom millwork are commonly quoted at 10–16 weeks from approved shop drawings, and slabs, appliances and specialty plumbing fittings each have their own clock. We order against the schedule rather than the calendar, so nothing sits in a warehouse while the crew waits — and nothing is missing on the day it is needed.

    The second is the building. Most Manhattan co-ops and condos limit construction to weekday business hours, restrict noisy work to a narrower window inside that, and shut the site down for holidays and building events. A schedule that ignores house rules is fiction. Ours is built from them.

    Renovating inside a Manhattan building

    In a house, you pull a permit and start. In a Manhattan apartment, the building is a second client, with its own review, its own rules and its own veto. Requirements vary building to building — here is the shape of it, and what we handle.

    1. Step 01

      Alteration agreement

      The board or managing agent issues the agreement that governs the work: permitted hours, protection requirements, insurance limits, deposits, and often restrictions on moving wet areas over a neighbor’s dry rooms. We read it before we price the job, because it sets both the cost and the schedule.

    2. Step 02

      Board and engineer review

      Drawings go to the board, and usually to the building’s engineer or architect, for comment — on structure, on riser tie-ins, on sound attenuation. Comments come back and the set is revised until it is approved. Budget real calendar time for this round; it is the most commonly underestimated part of the job.

    3. Step 03

      DOB filing and permits

      The architect or expediter files the alteration application with the Department of Buildings, and the licensed electrical and plumbing work is filed separately by those trades. In pre-1974 buildings an asbestos investigation, and the ACP-5 form that results from it, is typically required before a permit will issue.

    4. Step 04

      Insurance and vendor paperwork

      Certificates of insurance naming the building, the managing agent, the board and the owner — at the limits the alteration agreement requires — for us and for every subcontractor who walks through the door. This is the single most common reason a crew gets turned away in the lobby on day one.

    5. Step 05

      Protection, access and house rules

      Before demolition: masonite and board over lobby and corridor floors, protection in the service elevator cab and at door bucks, dust containment at the apartment entry, and a booked elevator window for every delivery and every carting run. Work hours, freight access and quiet periods are the building’s call, and we plan the crew around them.

    6. Step 06

      Inspections and sign-off

      Rough inspections have to pass before walls close. At the end, permits are signed off and the job is formally closed out with the building — the step owners most often discover was skipped years later, at resale.

    Working with your architect and designer

    Much of our work arrives as a finished set from an architect and an interior designer. Our job is to make the built apartment match those drawings — and to raise conflicts early, in a meeting, rather than late, inside a wall.

    Preconstruction review

    We read the set before pricing it and come back with constructability questions: ceiling heights against new ductwork, slab conditions under a relocated shower, whether a specified fixture can actually be served by the existing riser. All far cheaper to answer on paper.

    Line-item budgeting

    Pricing comes back by trade and by room, tied to the drawings and the finish schedule, so you can see where the money is and make choices before demolition instead of after.

    Shop drawings and submittals

    Millwork, stone and metal shop drawings are produced from field dimensions and routed to the designer for approval before anything is cut. Samples and submittals are logged and tracked through to a decision.

    Site meetings and a written record

    A standing walkthrough with the design team, with RFIs and change orders in writing. Field decisions get documented the day they are made, so the record matches what is actually behind the walls.

    Owner-supplied items

    Designer-procured fixtures, appliances, tile and hardware are received, inspected and stored against their install dates — with damage and shortages flagged while there is still time to replace them.

    No design in the field

    When a condition in the building makes a detail impossible, we bring options back to the architect and designer. We do not quietly substitute, and we do not build something other than what was drawn.
